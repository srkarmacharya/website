Nepal is a long way from Iran and the Persian Gulf. But if conflict there escalates, Nepali farmers would feel it quickly, not through the news but through prices. There are three routes.

## 1. Diesel

Nepal imports **all** of its petroleum, almost entirely through India. Around a fifth of the world's oil trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz, including a large share of India's crude. When that route is disrupted, India pays more, and so does Nepal.

On the farm, diesel is everywhere. Across the Indo-Gangetic Plains, including Nepal's Terai, **the great majority of irrigation pumps run on diesel**. So do the tractors, harvesters, threshers and transplanters that the government has worked hard to promote. Most of these machines are rented from private owners, who pass fuel costs straight on to farmers. A spike in oil prices raises a smallholder's costs at every step from ploughing to threshing, and it could stall years of progress on mechanisation.

## 2. Fertiliser and pesticides

Nepal imports almost all its chemical fertiliser and well over 90% of its pesticides, mostly through Indian ports and traders. Being landlocked already adds shipping, insurance and fuel surcharges before goods reach the border. After that come mountain roads and weak logistics.

Smallholders react to higher prices in a predictable way: **they apply less fertiliser**, and their rice, wheat and maize yields fall.

## 3. Remittances

Money sent home by migrant workers is equal to roughly **a quarter of Nepal's economy**, and about 40% of it comes from the Gulf, where close to two million Nepalis work. A wider war could slow construction there, cost jobs, and send workers home.

That money doesn't just pay for food and school fees. It pays for seed, fertiliser, machine rental and irrigation, and studies show it raises farm productivity. It also props up the foreign exchange reserves Nepal needs to buy fuel and fertiliser in the first place. A fall in remittances **at the same time** as a fuel and fertiliser shock would hit agriculture twice.

## What Nepal can do

Nepal can't control events in the Middle East, but it can be less exposed to them.

**Now:** keep diesel flowing during peak irrigation seasons, procure fertiliser early and from more than one source, watch input supplies closely, and fix logistics bottlenecks before a local shortage becomes a national crop loss.

**Over time:** move irrigation from diesel to **solar and electric pumps**, and help farmers get more from less fertiliser through conservation agriculture, precise nutrient management and healthier soils.

Building a more resilient farm sector is not only good development policy. For a country this exposed, it is economic common sense.
