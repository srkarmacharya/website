In Ramdhuni Municipality in Sunsari, farming is hard work for uncertain returns. Irrigation is patchy, good seed and fertiliser are hard to come by, and monkeys and blue bulls raid the fields. When we talked with farmers there in 2023 under the Rupantar project, one idea kept coming up: better cattle and more milk could add a steadier stream of income.

So the project, working through the local **Sagun Agriculture Cooperative**, began supporting about **50 farmers in Kalijhora and Lalpur**, half of them women. They got training on cattle management, visits to well-run dairy farms, and demonstrations of silage, which is fermented green fodder that can be stored for the lean season.

## What changed

A recent survey found that **more than two in three farmers had changed how they keep their cattle.** The most common change was in feeding, followed by better sheds and better animal health and hygiene. Nearly everyone who changed something reported **more milk and lower costs**.

Some benefits were ones we hadn't planned for. Many households used to cook porridge for their cattle every day. Switching to ready concentrate feed saved hours of work, cut firewood use, and meant less smoke in the kitchen.

Farmers also learned to use government services they hadn't known about, such as the district Agriculture Knowledge Centre and the veterinary hospital. As cooperative members they now have savings and credit, and support that will outlast the project. One farmer in Lalpur, Kumari Mahatara, grew her herd from a couple of milking cows to seven in a year.

## What's still hard

- **Disease and heat.** Lumpy skin disease swept through Koshi Province, and crossbred cows are struggling with summer heat.
- **Cows that don't get pregnant.** Repeated failed insemination costs farmers money, time and vet visits, and reliable vets are hard to find.
- **Insurance that doesn't pay.** Few farmers have cattle insurance. Agents rarely visit, and claims are slow.
- **Not everyone has joined in.** About one in four farmers hasn't adopted any new practice yet.
- **Too few silage machines.** Silage worked well in the demonstrations, but there are almost no machines in the area to make it.

The biggest problem, though, comes **after** the milking. The only organised buyer is a local collection centre that paid farmers **about Rs 58 a litre** on average last year. Five kilometres away, packaged milk sells for **Rs 90 to 110**, and farmers sometimes wait **up to three months** to be paid. Well-run dairy cooperatives in India return a far larger share of the shop price to the farmer.

## What would help

- Door-to-door fertility clinics, with portable ultrasound, run by the veterinary service and the agriculture university
- Simple advice on feed and mineral supplements, a cheap fix for many fertility problems
- A silage machine for the cooperative
- A fairer, more reliable buyer for the milk, and in the longer run, links to larger dairy cooperatives
- Research on making local dairy products, and on what consumers want
- Registering farmer groups formally so they can reach government livestock services, and training more local para-vets

Dairy can be a real livelihood here. But helping farmers produce more milk only pays off if they can sell it fairly and get paid on time.
