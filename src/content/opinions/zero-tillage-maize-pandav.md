On a foggy December evening in Morang, Pandav Mandal heard people in his village talking about a new way to grow maize: **sowing straight into the field without ploughing it first.**

At first it sounded absurd. But he started counting. The money he spent each year renting a rotavator to prepare the land could go to his daughter's school fees, or towards a cow.

## Hearing it from someone who'd seen it work

A team from Jeevan Bikas Samaj (JBS), the local partner of the Rupantar project, came to explain *zero tillage*. Tika Kumar Bisunke, who leads this work in Morang, told the village how it had gone in nearby villages the year before. Farmers had saved money, the soil had held more moisture, and it had kept more organic matter. To make trying it easier, the project offered the zero-till drill **free of charge** to farmers who brought their own seed and fertiliser.

## When the weather didn't cooperate

Pandav started small, on **three kattha** of land. But unseasonal rain had left the soil wet and sticky. Mud clogged the drill's outlets, and he couldn't tell whether the seed was actually going into the ground.

Many people would have given up there. Pandav talked it through with Tika and **changed the method** for the rest of his land, another seven kattha. He used the machine to open the rows and place the fertiliser, then dropped the maize seed into the furrows **by hand**. It was more work, but he could see that every seed was where it should be.

That night, he said, he slept well.

## Why this matters

New farming practices rarely arrive fully formed. Farmers test them on a small plot, hit a problem, and adapt. The adapted version is often the one that sticks.

With rainfall getting less predictable, this kind of **flexibility**, backed by advice from someone nearby, may matter as much as the technology itself. Supporting farmers to adapt, rather than expecting them to follow a fixed recipe, is one of the most practical ways to make farming both more productive and more sustainable.
