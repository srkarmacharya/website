Drive through the eastern Terai today and you'll see more farm machines than ever: combine harvesters, rice transplanters, and businesses that rent them out by the hour. Farmers are taking up new tools faster than before, and the reason is simple. As wages rise, a machine is often cheaper than a team of hired hands.

The national numbers tell the same story. The latest agricultural census (2021/22) counts **more than 1.6 million farmers using tractors**, close to double the figure a decade earlier. Around half a million use power tillers. Meanwhile, the number using the traditional iron plough fell from over 850,000 to about 510,000.

## A small example from Morang

Under the Rupantar project, we introduced a **zero-tillage seed-and-fertiliser drill** for winter maize in two villages in Morang. It sows straight into unploughed soil.

Farmers liked it. Chotu Sada, a maize farmer, told us he needed fewer workers for land preparation and sowing, spent less on irrigation, and had fewer weeds to deal with. Birendra Mandal said hand-planting used to take days, time he can now spend on other work. Women, who usually do most of the planting and weeding, gained the most from that.

An agronomist from the Agriculture and Forestry University also noticed that maize roots grew deeper and stronger in the untilled soil. Over the years, zero tillage should also build up soil organic matter.

## The other side

Not everyone was pleased. Lila Devi Mandal, who earns a living as a hired farm worker, put it plainly: the machine works well, but it does her job. Farmers say they're short of labour, yet labourers fear the work will disappear entirely. Most of these worries came from **women and poorer households**, who make up most of the hired farm workforce.

## Which comes first?

The usual story is that young people leave for the cities and the Gulf, labour gets scarce and expensive, and farmers turn to machines. People leave because farming doesn't pay, farm wages are low, better jobs exist elsewhere, and neighbours with a migrant in the family seem to be doing better.

But the story may also run the other way. **Machines may themselves push people out of farm work**, especially those with the least land and the fewest options. Each change feeds the other.

Something similar happened with industrialisation, and it's happening again with AI: technology reshapes who works and where. Farm machinery and migration deserve the same careful look, with attention to **who gains and who loses, by gender and by class**. Zero-tillage maize in Morang could be a textbook case to start with.
