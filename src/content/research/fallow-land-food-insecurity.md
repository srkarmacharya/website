## The question

The Eastern Gangetic Plains, which take in Nepal's Terai, eastern India and northern Bangladesh, have some of the most fertile land in South Asia. They are also home to one of the world's largest concentrations of rural poverty. Yet every year, many farmers grow nothing at all on part of their land for a whole season.

We wanted to know three things: how common this *fallowing* is, why farmers do it, and whether it actually leaves families with less to eat.

## What we did

We surveyed **1,440 farm households in 72 villages** across India, Nepal and Bangladesh. We used statistical models to find what predicts the decision to leave land fallow. To measure food insecurity, we used the FAO's Food Insecurity Experience Scale, a standard set of questions about skipping meals, eating less, or running out of food.

Households that leave land fallow differ from those that don't in many ways, so a simple comparison could mislead. We therefore used a causal method that weights households to compare like with like before estimating the effect.

## What we found

- **Fallowing is common in winter.** About **31% of households** left land fallow in the *rabi* (winter) season, compared with about 10% in the monsoon season. India had the highest rate, at **41%** in winter.
- **The reasons are practical, not lazy.** Across the region, farmers pointed to a lack of irrigation, a lack of capital, labour shortages and climate risk. In **Nepal, lack of irrigation was the main reason in both seasons.**
- **Empty fields mean emptier plates.** Leaving land fallow raised the chance of a household being food insecure by **9 percentage points in Nepal** and **15 percentage points in India**.

> An unplanted field is not just lost output. It shows up as skipped meals in the household that owns it.

## Why it matters

Governments in the region already want to bring winter fallow back into production. Our results suggest the payoff goes beyond national grain totals: it reaches household food security directly. They also suggest where to start. In Nepal that means irrigation first. Across the region, it also means credit, crop insurance, direct cash support to farmers, and extension advice tailored to local conditions rather than one-size-fits-all programmes.
