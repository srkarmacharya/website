## The question

Farming in the Eastern Gangetic Plains is getting more knowledge-intensive. Markets are changing, the climate is less predictable, and new technologies keep arriving. But small farmers can only act on what they hear about. **Where do they get information today, and where would they like to get it from?**

## What we did

We surveyed **1,440 households** in Nepal (Koshi Province), India (West Bengal) and Bangladesh (Rangpur). We asked about both farming information and *off-farm* information, such as jobs and businesses beyond the farm. For each, we asked which sources people use now and which they would prefer in future.

## What we found

- **Neighbours and agrovets fill the gap.** In all three countries, the most common sources were fellow villagers and *agrovets* (local input shops). In Nepal, **90% of households** got farming information from village peers.
- **Nepal leans on local officers and model farmers.** Nepali farmers had better access to local agriculture officers (25%) than farmers in the other two countries. Many also learned from model farmers (39%) and farmer groups (28%).
- **People want something more formal.** Asked about the future, many respondents chose government sources such as the Department of Agriculture and local agricultural officers. For information about off-farm work and business, **70% of Nepali respondents preferred government extension agents**.
- **Training beats leaflets.** Hands-on training was the most preferred way to receive information.
- **No single channel works for everyone.** Preferences differed by country and by topic, so a mix is needed.

## Why it matters

The current system works largely by default: farmers rely on whoever is nearby. The study argues for a **pluralistic extension system**, in which government, private businesses and community groups each do what they do best, and for investing in training rather than one-way messaging. That would put useful information within reach of the farmers who need it most.
