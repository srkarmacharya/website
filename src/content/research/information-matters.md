## The question

Farmers in the Eastern Gangetic Plains, across Bangladesh, India and Nepal, are increasingly encouraged to diversify: to grow new crops or add income from beyond the farm. Whether they can depends partly on what they know, and on who they learn it from.

Information does more than guide one farmer's decision. It shapes what a whole region grows, who people trust, and how communities relate to one another. **So how does the information system around farmers actually work, and how well?**

## What we did

We held **36 focus group discussions**, with men and women farmers in separate groups, across the three countries. Holding them separately let us hear how access to information differs between men and women.

We compared the main channels farmers rely on:

- **Public extension services** run by government
- **Informal community networks**: neighbours, relatives and fellow farmers
- **Private agrovets**: the local shops that sell seeds, fertiliser and pesticides, and often give advice along with them

For each channel we looked at its strengths, its limits and how well it fits local conditions. We paid particular attention to social and cultural norms, trust, institutional support and access to technology.

## What we found

Informal and relational networks are at the centre of the system. **Farmer-to-farmer exchange and conversations with input sellers** carry much of the information that reaches smallholders. These relationships can either enable or restrict a household's ability to diversify its livelihood.

## Why it matters

Programmes that promote diversification often assume information flows through official channels. This study suggests it mostly flows through people farmers already know and trust. Extension efforts are more likely to work if they build on those networks, including agrovets and farmer-to-farmer learning, rather than around them.

Conceptually, the paper treats agriculture as a *complex adaptive system*, a web of many actors learning from each other. It draws on Social Network Theory and the Agricultural Knowledge and Innovation Systems (AKIS) framework.
